How a robot brain reads a photo — and what a sticker can't do to it

A robot arm looks at a table and decides how to move. We put a probe inside its brain and watched a single photo travel all the way to the motors. Then we taped a sticker onto the scene to see what broke.

Robot brain: OpenVLA-7B Job: pick up the soup, put it in the basket Everything here: measured, not guessed

1

Two things in, seven numbers out

the whole robot, in one line

Forget the robot for a second and think about a very literal-minded assistant. Every third of a second you hand them exactly two things:

What goes in

A photo — one 224×224 pixel snapshot from a camera above the table. That's a small image, about the size of a postage stamp on your screen.

A sentence — the instruction, in plain English: “pick up the alphabet soup and place it in the basket.”

And they hand you back seven numbers. Not a plan, not a sentence — seven numbers, which are wired straight to the arm:

The first six say move a little this way, tilt a little that way. The seventh says how open the gripper is (0.996 ≈ wide open). Those are the real numbers from the first frame of a real run — tiny, because the arm is just settling.

Do that 280 times in a row and you get a robot that picks something up. Our question is simple: if an attacker can change the photo, how much of that behaviour can they steal?

2

The photo gets cut into 256 tiles

stage ① — vision encoder

A brain built out of language can't read pixels. So the first thing that happens is that the photo is chopped into a 16×16 grid of tiles — 256 of them, each 14×14 pixels. Every tile gets summarised into a long list of numbers describing what's in it.

Why 2176 numbers per tile? Because the robot looks at every tile twice, with two different sets of eyes, and staples the results together:

Two eyes on every tile

SigLIP → 1152 numbers. Trained on images paired with captions, so it is good at what things are — “that's a soup can.”

DINOv2 → 1024 numbers. Trained on images alone, so it is good at where things are — edges, shapes, layout.

1152 + 1024 = 2176 numbers describing each tile.

So the photo has become a block of 256 × 2176 numbers. Or: 256 little descriptions, one per tile.

3

A translator turns tiles into “words”

stage ② — the projector

The language part of the robot only understands one kind of thing: a token, a list of exactly 4096 numbers, which is how it stores a word. The tile descriptions are 2176 numbers long, in a completely different format. They don't fit.

So there's a small translator in between — three quick layers of arithmetic:

The translator (an MLP projector)

2176 → 8704 → 4096 → 4096

Feed it a tile description, get back something the language model will happily read as if it were a word. Do that 256 times and the photo has become 256 image “words”.

They aren't English words. Nobody can look one up in a dictionary. They're positions in the same 4096-dimensional space where the model keeps “basket” and “pick” — close enough that the language model can't tell it's being handed pictures.

You can see the translator doing its job in the raw statistics. The vision encoder's output is wild — a few values as large as +284. After the translator, everything sits in a tidy range that matches real word-tokens:

“Spread” is how far the numbers scatter from the average. Notice it drops from 3.3 to 0.44 — the translator is squeezing the loud, spiky vision numbers into the calm range the language model expects. And notice that six channels of pixels go in, not three: the photo is normalised twice, once for each set of eyes.

4

Photo and sentence become one long line

stage ③ — 282 tokens

Here is the part that matters most, and it's much simpler than people expect. The image words and the real words are just glued end to end into a single line, and that line is what the language model reads:

[ START ] + [ 256 image words ] + [ 25 sentence words ] = 282

One start marker, then the photo, then the instruction. That's the entire input. No separate “vision system” talking to a “language system” — one line of 282 tokens, read left to right.

The model then writes seven more tokens on the end — and those seven get decoded into the seven motor numbers from step 1. That's it. That's the whole robot.

Because the language model reads left to right, the seven action tokens at the end can “look back” at everything before them, including all 256 image words. That's the road from a pixel to a motor — and it's the road an attacker has to use.

5

Now we tape a sticker on the table

the experiment — same brain, two photos

We ran the exact same instruction twice: once with a clean photo, once with a photo that has a printed label stuck in the scene. Then we compared the two lines of 282 tokens, token by token.

To compare, we use similarity: 1.000 means byte-for-byte identical, and lower means more different. Press the toggle.

Two things happened, and the second one is the surprise.

Similarity 1.0000 is not “almost the same”. It is identical. Every single one of those 25 numbers-per-token, in all 25 tokens, came out the same as before. Painting words onto the table does not edit the instruction — there's no path in the wiring from the photo into the sentence.

And yet the robot's motion changed hard. The sideways command went from −0.003 to −0.293 — about 100× bigger — while the gripper command didn't move at all. The arm lurches sideways with its hand still hanging open.

6

Breaking a robot vs. bossing it around

the point of the whole thing

Those two results together answer the question we started with, and they draw a sharp line between two very different attacks:

Two kinds of attack

Denial of service — “break it”. Wreck the robot's sense of where things are so it fails the job. Lurching sideways with an open gripper is exactly this. This works.

Hijack — “boss it around”. Make the robot smoothly and deliberately do your task instead: pick up a different object, put it somewhere else. That needs a coherent new goal, not a shove. A readable sticker cannot do this.

Fogging up someone's glasses will absolutely stop them cooking dinner.

It will never make them cook your dinner instead. To do that you'd have to get at the recipe — and the recipe is in the block we measured at 1.0000.

This is a fact about how the robot is built, not about how hard we tried. You could write the world's most persuasive sentence on a sign and hold it in front of the camera: it still lands in the photo block, and the instruction block still comes out identical. There is no reader turning scene text back into language. The image can shove the motors, but it cannot rewrite the goal.

So a research team hunting for a hijack by trying prettier, bolder, more legible signs is searching a space where the answer provably isn't. That's a useful thing to know before spending a month of GPU time on it.

7

Then how would someone hijack it?

and how you'd catch them

Not by writing something readable. By computing a pattern.

The robot is made of arithmetic, so you can run it backwards and ask: which pixel should I nudge, and in which direction, to make the seventh output number go up? Repeat a few thousand times and you get a patch that looks to us like coloured static, but which lands in exactly the right spot in the model's 4096-number space to steer it on purpose. That's a real attack, it needs full access to the model's internals, and it's a separate research question — the probe in this article is what shows you precisely where such an attack would have to attach.

The defence falls out of the same measurement. The attack leaves a very specific fingerprint, all three parts at once:

The fingerprint of a visual injection

1. The instruction is untouched — similarity exactly 1.0000.

2. The photo tokens jumped — similarity fell to 0.729.

3. The motor command jumped with it — 100× on one axis.

Nothing in normal operation looks like that. A watchdog sitting between the eyes and the language model can check for it on every single frame — a photo that argues with its own instruction.

The real names for things

so the vocabulary transfers

token
One item in the line the model reads — here, a list of 4096 numbers. A word is a token; so is one image tile.
patch
One of the 256 tiles the photo is cut into. 14×14 pixels each.
vision encoder
The two sets of eyes (SigLIP + DINOv2) that summarise each tile into 2176 numbers.
projector
The translator. Three layers of arithmetic, 2176 → 4096, so tiles can sit in a line beside words.
cosine similarity
The comparison we used. 1.000 = identical direction, 0 = unrelated. It ignores overall size and asks only “are these pointing the same way?”
7-DoF action
Seven degrees of freedom: three for moving, three for rotating, one for the gripper.
VLA
Vision-Language-Action model. The family this robot brain belongs to: sees, reads, acts.
DoS / hijack
Denial of service = make it fail. Hijack = make it obey you. Very different threat levels.
